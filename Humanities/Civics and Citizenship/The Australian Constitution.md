# The Australian Constitution

Until 1901, Australia consisted of six self-governing British colonies. Each had its own laws and government. Before federation, they had to agree on the powers the new Commonwealth would receive and the powers the states would retain.

A constitution is a set of rules defining a system of government and the limits on its operation. The Australian Constitution is Australia's foundational legal document. Each state also has its own constitution.

It:

- established a federal system of government in Australia;
- granted specified law-making powers to the Commonwealth Parliament;
- created the structure of [[Houses of Parliament|Parliament]]; and
- created the High Court to resolve disputes about the Constitution's meaning.

The commonwealth parliament can only make laws in the areas specifically stated in the constitution.

Section 109 states that when valid Commonwealth and state laws are inconsistent, the Commonwealth law prevails to the extent of the inconsistency.

Referendums have rarely succeeded in changing the Constitution. Power has also shifted from the states to the Commonwealth through other mechanisms and High Court interpretations.

The High Court can determine whether a government has acted *ultra vires*, meaning beyond its constitutional powers. This supports the [[Separation of Powers]] and [[Rule of Law]].

Examples include the Tasmanian Dam case (external affairs power), the Brislan case (postal, telegraphic, telephonic and "other like services") and the Work Choices case (corporations power).



## Tasmanian Dam case

The Tasmanian government sought to build a dam on the Franklin River. The Commonwealth attempted to stop the project using legislation based partly on its external affairs power. The High Court upheld the Commonwealth legislation.
